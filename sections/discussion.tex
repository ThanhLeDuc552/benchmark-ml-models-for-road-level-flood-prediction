\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Classification Scores and Routing Decisions}
The best classifier was not the best router. Random forest had the highest AUC-PR, yet at $\lambda=5$ the MLP and XGBoost routes crossed less flooding. Two effects explain this. First, the five nonlinear models are statistically indistinguishable in AUC-PR, so small differences in score need not carry over to routes. Second, a fixed $\lambda$ converts each model's probabilities into costs on a different scale: models with higher probabilities on flooded segments detour more, which lowers exposure but raises travel time. When the models are compared at equal travel-time cost (Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto}), their curves lie close together. For practitioners, models for flood-aware routing should therefore be compared on exposure--time curves, or at least by recall and calibration, rather than by AUC at a single operating point.

\subsection{Validation Practice}
Random cross-validation inflated AUC-ROC for every model, by up to 0.094, and made random forest appear near-perfect (0.992). The inflation was largest for the tree ensembles, which can memorize local terrain values that neighboring segments share. Studies that report very high accuracy for urban flood susceptibility with random splits~\cite{nguyen2026,hoang2025,yuan2023} should therefore be read with caution, and spatial blocking should be the default for road-level benchmarks.

\subsection{Flood Drivers}
All models ranked terrain first, led by depression depth and TPI, followed by distance to the Han River. This agrees with the hydraulic mechanism in Da Nang, where high river levels blocked the outfalls and water ponded in low-lying streets~\cite{doan2023}. However, the labels come from a hydraulic simulation driven by terrain, so part of this agreement is expected by construction: the models partly learn to reproduce the simulation from the same terrain. Network features added little, and road connectivity did not measurably improve GraphSAGE.

\subsection{Limitations}
Six limitations apply. First, the labels are simulated rather than observed, so the models predict where the HEC-RAS model floods, and their accuracy against real flooding depends on how well that model was validated. Second, the study uses one event in one district, so the rainfall features are constant and the models identify flood-prone segments rather than forecasting floods for other storms. Third, a 30~m DEM may miss street-scale depressions. Fourth, hyperparameters were fixed rather than tuned, and results come from a single seed, so small differences between models should not be over-interpreted. Fifth, the routing compares models at one $\lambda$ in Table~\ref{tab:routing}, which mixes prediction quality with probability scale. Sixth, 94.7\% of trips cross flooding even with perfect knowledge, so route choice alone cannot keep vehicles out of the water during an event of this size.
